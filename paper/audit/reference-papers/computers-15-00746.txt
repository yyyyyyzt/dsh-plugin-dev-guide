Abstract
Phishing attacks have continued to increase globally over the past several decades and
remain a major entry point for multi-vector cyberattacks. As conventional single-method
detection techniques often struggle to identify increasingly sophisticated phishing attempts,
researchers have increasingly focused on AI-based hybrid detection approaches. This
systematic review, conducted in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 statement, provides
a focused synthesis of these approaches. A comprehensive search was conducted across
seven academic databases and one scholarly search engine. Three reviewers independently
screened the records, extracted the data, and conducted the methodological quality as-
sessment, with disagreements resolved through discussion and consensus. Following a
multi-stage screening process, 18 peer-reviewed studies published between January 2020
and June 2026 were included. The methodological quality of each study was evaluated
using a structured quality-assessment checklist. The included studies were synthesized nar-
ratively with emphasis on hybridization techniques, data modalities, model architectures,
evaluation metrics, strengths, limitations, and future research directions. The findings indi-
cate that AI-based hybrid approaches generally achieved strong benchmark performance
and improved robustness against the phishing scenarios evaluated within the included
studies. However, limitations were identified in relation to explainability, generalizability,
computational complexity, and real-time deployment. The review also identified con-
tinuing challenges involving the trade-off between accuracy and efficiency, dependence
on unimodal or benchmark datasets, limited reproducibility reporting, and insufficient
real-world validation. Future research should prioritize lightweight and fairness-aware
hybrid architectures, multimodal data integration, continual learning, and stronger model
interpretability to support reliable and scalable deployment in real-world environments.

1. Introduction
In this rapidly changing digital era, technology advances at a rate that frequently
surpasses our capacity to address the increasing prevalence of cybercrimes [ 1]. Now,
cybercriminals have become increasingly active and equipped with advanced strategies,
adversely affecting both persons and enterprises. At present, instead of depending on a
singular technique, cybercriminals often employ multi-vector strategies [2–4], complicating
the detection and mitigation of these threats. In multi-vector strategies, phishing continues
to be a predominant tactic employed by cybercriminals, primarily functioning as the entry
point for launching more extensive attacks. Commonly termed “human hacking” [5], phish-
ing is characterized as a cyber-enabled crime in which perpetrators utilize psychological
manipulation, merging social engineering with technical deceit, to mislead individuals or
organizations into revealing confidential information [6]. Where other cybercrimes entail
direct manipulation of systems, phishing targets human psychology and exploits the cogni-
tive vulnerabilities of the person behind the system [7,8]. So, despite being an old technique,
phishing remains highly effective and a preferred method for initiating cyber-attacks due
to its ability to bypass technological defenses by exploiting human behavior.
Furthermore, the emergence of generative artificial intelligence (GenAI), particularly
Large Language Models (LLMs), has further exacerbated this issue [9]. These advanced
technologies enable cybercriminals to craft highly realistic phishing content, execute tar-
geted attacks, and personalize their approaches [10]. Studies by [11,12] found that LLMs,
such as ChatGPT and Claude, are capable of generating human-like text, engaging in
coherent conversations, and performing linguistic tasks at superhuman levels, thereby
making phishing attempts more sophisticated, harder to detect, and faster with less cost.
Consequently, the incidence of phishing attacks continues to rise over recent years [13]. This
continued proliferation of phishing attacks underscores the adaptability and dynamicity
of cybercriminals, making phishing one of the most organized cybercrimes of the twenty-
first century [14]. Consequently, it has recently gained increasing focus as an alarming
cybercrime globally, from individuals to organizations.
Previous review studies have extensively covered various aspects of phishing, high-
lighting the multifaceted nature of the problem and the ongoing evolution of combat
strategies. Studies such as [ 1,13,15–17], and [ 18] focused on history, lifecycle, motiva-
tions, taxonomies, various attack vectors, and mitigation strategies. A subset of these,
like [15] and [ 17], also discussed existing countermeasures and the challenges faced in
combating phishing. Another category of reviews concentrated specifically on detection
strategies. Some, like [ 19–22] and [ 23], analyzed and compared different detection ap-
proaches, including rule-based, list-based, heuristic-based, visual-similarity-based, and
various machine-learning-based techniques. More recent reviews, such as [24–30] and [31],
delved into the specific application of advanced techniques like deep learning, graph-
based, network-embedding-based, eye-tracking-based, and other AI-enabled approaches
for phishing detection. On the other hand, few review articles focused on the human
element of phishing, addressing user susceptibility and the effectiveness of training and
awareness programs [32–35].
Although previous reviews have examined AI-based phishing detection techniques
broadly, they primarily focus on individual learning paradigms such as machine learning,
deep learning, or specific detection modalities. However, the increasing adoption of hybrid
architectures that combine multiple models, heterogeneous feature spaces, multimodal
information sources, and operational components introduces unique design considerations,
deployment challenges, and evaluation issues that remain insufficiently synthesized. To
address this gap, this study spotlights AI-based hybrid phishing detection strategies, exam-
ining their strengths and weaknesses, highlighting existing controversies, and outlining

2. Study Methodology
This systematic review aims to provide a focused overview of AI-based hybrid phish-
ing detection strategies. To ensure methodological rigor and transparency, the review was
conducted and is reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic
reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 statement [36]. The completed PRISMA 2020
checklist, indicating the section and page on which each item is reported, is provided as
a Supplementary File accompanying this submission. This study was not prospectively
registered in a public protocol registry (e.g., PROSPERO), as focused reviews of this scope
are not eligible for registration on that platform; consequently, checklist items relating to
protocol registration are reported as not applicable. Because a narrative synthesis (rather
than a meta-analysis) was performed, checklist items concerning effect measures, statistical
heterogeneity, meta-analysis methods, and certainty-of-evidence (GRADE) assessment are
also reported as not applicable.
2.1. Eligibility Criteria
This study applied predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria to ensure the relevance,
reliability, and quality of the included studies. Peer-reviewed journal articles, conference
papers, and book chapters published between January 2020 and June 2026 were consid-
ered eligible for inclusion. The year 2020 was selected as the lower temporal boundary
because this review specifically focuses on the contemporary generation of AI-based hybrid
phishing detection approaches, including ensemble, multimodal, explainable, and adaptive
architectures. Earlier phishing-detection studies and reviews were considered in estab-
lishing the background and research gap; however, restricting the systematic synthesis to
studies published from 2020 onward enabled a focused assessment of recent developments
in hybrid AI-based phishing detection while reducing overlap with earlier broad reviews
of phishing-detection techniques.
Studies were included if they addressed AI-based hybrid phishing detection strate-
gies, including their applications, effectiveness, strengths, and challenges. For this review,
hybrid phishing detection was operationally defined as a phishing detection framework
that integrates at least two functionally distinct detection or representation components
within a unified detection pipeline, where the integrated components jointly contribute
to feature representation, learning, classification, or the final phishing detection decision.
The integration of these components had to be explicitly described in the methodology of
the original study. Under this definition, hybrid approaches primarily included the inte-
gration of multiple AI models, heterogeneous feature representations, and/or multimodal
data sources. Examples include combining heterogeneous URL, HTML, DOM, email, or
semantic features; integrating multiple machine learning or deep learning models through

ensemble, stacking, cascading, or related mechanisms; and fusing distinct information
modalities such as textual, visual, structural, metadata, or behavioral information. To
ensure consistency in study selection, the presence of an additional technique alone was not
considered sufficient evidence of hybridization. In particular, a study was not classified as
hybrid solely because it employed an explainability technique, conventional optimization
algorithm, class-balancing procedure, data augmentation method, or operational adap-
tation mechanism together with a single detection model. Such techniques were treated
as supporting characteristics unless they constituted a functionally distinct component
explicitly integrated into the phishing detection pipeline.
Studies focusing exclusively on single-method phishing detection, non-peer-reviewed
publications, and publications lacking original research contributions, such as editorials,
opinions, and commentaries, were excluded. Studies with insufficient methodological
detail or insufficient information relevant to the analysis of hybrid detection strategies
were also excluded from the selection pool. All included studies were grouped into a
single narrative synthesis (Sections 3.1–3.5); no separate sub-syntheses or meta-analytic
pooling were performed because of the heterogeneity of study designs, datasets, and
evaluation metrics.

2.4. Selection Process
The study-selection process followed a systematic, multi-stage procedure conducted
independently by three reviewers (Maruf Ahmed Tamal, Kazi Imtiaz Kabir, and Rubaiyat Is-
lam). The database and search-engine searches yielded 41,301 records, and an additional six
records were identified through manual reference-list searching, resulting in41,307 records
in total.
Before formal screening, the retrieved records were consolidated and checked for
duplicate entries using available bibliographic information, including article titles, authors,
publication details, and DOI information where available. This process resulted in the
removal of 3219 duplicate records. The remaining 38,088 records were then subjected to
preliminary filtering based on bibliographic information and search-result metadata to
remove records that were clearly outside the predefined scope of the review. At this stage,
37,017 records were excluded: 16,091 were clearly unrelated to phishing detection, 9781 fell
outside the specified publication period, 7201 did not meet the eligible publication-type
criteria, and 3944 failed to satisfy other basic eligibility requirements defined in Section 2.1.
Each record was assigned to a single primary exclusion category to avoid double counting.
Following duplicate removal and preliminary filtering, 1071 records remained for formal
title and abstract screening.

2.5. Data Extraction and Synthesis
Data were independently extracted from the 18 included studies by all three reviewers
using a standardized, piloted extraction form covering study methodology, AI model(s)
employed (hybrid strategy), dataset(s) used, evaluation metrics, key findings, reported
strengths and limitations, and identified future research directions. In addition, the extrac-
tion process recorded the principal hybridization characteristics of each included study
according to the operational definition established in Section 2.1, including the integration
of heterogeneous feature representations, multiple AI models, and/or multimodal data
sources, as applicable. Extracted data were cross-checked between reviewers, and dis-
crepancies were resolved through discussion and, where necessary, re-examination of the
source article. No automation tools were used for data extraction. Where a study reported
results across multiple datasets or configurations, the extraction form captured the range or
best-performing configuration. The extracted data were subsequently synthesized using a
narrative synthesis approach, following the framework of Campbell et al. [37], highlighting
recurrent themes and patterns across the included studies. The synthesis was structured
to address the study’s research objectives, including an analysis of state-of-the-art hybrid
anti-phishing strategies, their strengths and limitations, existing debates, and potential
future developments (Sections 3.2–3.5). To assess the influence of methodological quality
on the review findings, a qualitative sensitivity analysis was performed by excluding the
only low-quality study and reassessing whether the principal hybridization strategies, per-
formance trends, methodological limitations, and research challenges remained supported
by the other included studies.
2.6. Methodological Quality Assessment
Because the included studies primarily involved algorithmic evaluations rather than
clinical, epidemiological, or observational research, established risk-of-bias instruments
designed for clinical and observational studies, such as Cochrane RoB 2, ROBINS-I, and the
Newcastle-Ottawa Scale, were not directly applicable. Therefore, methodological quality
was assessed using a seven-item checklist developed specifically for this review. The seven
criteria assessed (C1) clarity of research objectives, (C2) adequacy of dataset reporting, (C3)
appropriateness of evaluation methodology, (C4) comparison with baseline or existing
methods, (C5) explicit discussion of study limitations, (C6) availability of code or data
supporting reproducibility, and (C7) evidence of large-scale or real-world validation. These
criteria were selected to capture research transparency, evaluation rigor, reproducibility,
and generalizability. Each criterion was scored as 1 (fully met), 0.5 (partially met), or 0 (not
met), producing a maximum total score of 7. Studies scoring 6.0–7.0 were classified as high
quality, 4.0–5.5 as moderate quality, and below 4.0 as low quality. These review-specific
thresholds were selected to distinguish studies meeting nearly all assessment criteria from
those with more substantial methodological or reporting deficiencies. All seven criteria
received equal weight, and the categories were intended for descriptive interpretation
rather than as externally validated quality classifications. Three reviewers independently
assessed all 18 included studies. Inter-rater agreement was evaluated using Cohen’s kappa,

3. Results and Discussions
3.1. Study Selection and Quality Assessment
Of the 41,301 records identified through the searched sources, together with six addi-
tional records identified through manual reference-list searching, 1071 records remained
after duplicate removal and preliminary relevance filtering (Figure 1). Following indepen-
dent title and abstract screening by three reviewers, 964 records were excluded because
they did not address AI-based hybrid phishing detection, leaving 107 reports for which
full texts were sought. Of these, 70 reports could not be retrieved, and the remaining
37 full-text reports were assessed against the predefined eligibility criteria. Nineteen re-
ports were excluded following full-text assessment for the reasons summarized in Table 2.
Consequently, 18 studies met all eligibility criteria and were included in the narrative
synthesis. The methodological quality of the included studies was evaluated using the
seven-item checklist described in Section 2.6 and Appendix A (Table A1), with detailed
scores presented in Table 3. Among the 18 studies, 16 (88.9%) were classified as high quality,
one (5.6%) as moderate quality, and one (5.6%) as low quality. Shaukat et al. [42] received
a moderate-quality classification (5.0/7), whereas Alsubaei et al. [40] received the lowest
score (3.5/7). The latter study exhibited incomplete dataset reporting, limited evaluation-
methodology details, an absence of explicit discussion of study limitations, insufficient
reproducibility resources, and limited evidence of broader validation. These deficiencies
warrant caution when interpreting its reported performance improvements, particularly
those associated with SMOTE-based class balancing and hyperparameter optimization.
Overall, most studies clearly stated their research objectives, adequately described their
datasets, employed appropriate evaluation procedures, and compared their proposed mod-
els with baseline or previously published approaches. The evaluation strategies included
train–test splitting, cross-validation, ablation analysis, cross-dataset testing, adversarial
evaluation, and deployment-oriented experiments. Nevertheless, reproducibility remained
a common methodological weakness, as several studies did not provide complete source
code, processed datasets, trained models, or configuration files required for independent
replication. Furthermore, evaluations conducted on individual benchmark datasets or un-
der controlled experimental conditions limited the generalizability of reported performance
to evolving real-world phishing attacks. Some studies also provided insufficient discussion
of their methodological constraints.

3.2. Hybrid Anti-Phishing Strategies
This review includes 18 peer-reviewed studies published between 2020 and 2026, all
of which met the predefined inclusion criteria. The selected articles differ in their levels of
hybridization, data modalities, model architectures, fusion strategies, evaluation settings,
and performance metrics. Collectively, they represent the current development of AI-based
hybrid phishing detection, ranging from feature-level and mod-el-level combinations to
multimodal, explainable, adaptive, and deployment-oriented frameworks. Table 4 presents
a comparative summary of the included studies, high-lighting their hybrid strategies, ob-
jectives, key findings, strengths, limitations, unresolved challenges, and future research
directions. The selected 18 studies demonstrate a clear shift from single-method phishing
detection toward hybrid systems that combine complementary data sources, learning al-
gorithms, and, in some cases, additional operational components. To provide a consistent
taxonomy, the hybridization characteristics observed across the included studies can be
organized into three primary forms of hybridization—feature-level, model-level, and mul-
timodal hybridization—together with an additional pipeline- and lifecycle-level dimension
describing supporting operational characteristics. The three primary forms correspond
directly to the operational definition established in Section 2.1 because they involve the ex-
plicit integration of multiple functionally distinct feature representations, learning models,

common strategy, where heterogeneous features such as URL lexical characteristics, HTML
content, DOM structures, email metadata, sender reputation, and semantic embeddings are
jointly used [38,39,46,54]. These approaches generally improve detection by combining syn-
tactic, contextual, and structural signals that may not be sufficiently informative when used
independently. For example, the hybrid CNN–LSTM model in [54] integrates email text
with metadata, URL characteristics, and sender-reputation information, enabling the model
to capture both local linguistic cues and longer sequential dependencies. Second, model-
level hybridization is widely adopted through ensemble learning, stacking, super-learning,
and parallel deep architectures [38,40,41,43–45,47]. These approaches combine multiple
classifiers or specialized branches to exploit their complementary strengths. Several studies
report-ed accuracies above 99%, while learned stacking and super-learner architectures
produced measurable gains over individual base models. The new multimodal expert sys-
tem in [52], for example, processes email text through DistilBERT, URL features through an
MLP , webpage screenshots through ResNet-18, and behavioural logs through TF-IDF with
Logistic Regression. Their outputs are combined using a Logistic Regression meta-learner
in a late-fusion design. Third, multimodal hybridization has become more prominent.
Earlier studies mainly combined URL and HTML features, while newer systems integrate
textual, visual, structural, metadata, and behavioural modalities [42,51–53,55]. The frame-
work in [53] combines textual, visual, and metadata representations through cross-attention
and supplements learned representations with symbolic rules. Similarly [55], employs four
modality-specific branches based on CatBoost, CNN1D, CodeBERT, and EfficientNet-B7,
followed by weighted late fusion. These studies show that multimodal integration can
detect phishing evidence distributed across different channels, including brand imitation,
malicious page structure, suspicious text, and URL manipulation. Finally, a smaller group
of studies extends hybrid phishing detection beyond the core feature, model, and modality
integration toward the broader pipeline and lifecycle level, incorporating class balancing,
feature optimization, adversarial augmentation, explainability, concept-drift handling, con-
tinual learning, fairness auditing, and human-in-the-loop adaptation [40,41,46,48–50,53,55].
These mechanisms should be interpreted as supporting or operational characteristics of
hybrid detection frameworks rather than independent evidence of hybridization when used
alone with a single detection model. Their relevance lies in extending the capabilities of an
already integrated detection framework toward robustness, interpretability, adaptability,
and real-world deployment. The NeuroSym-bolicPhishDefend framework in [53] combines
multimodal learning, explicit rule-based reasoning, diffusion-based adversarial augmenta-
tion, SHAP explanations, and online learning with a replay buffer. In contrast [55], focuses
on operational deployment through a micro-service architecture integrated with security
operations infrastructure, while also incorporating SHAP , Grad-CAM, attention analysis,
and LLM-generated explanations. Study [48] similarly integrates deep learning and opti-
mization components through a GAN- and transformer-based feature-learning framework,
while [49] addresses automated lifecycle management and adaptive phishing detection
through a hybrid MLOps framework. These developments indicate that recent research is
moving beyond classification accuracy toward resilience, explainability, adaptability, and
real-world usability.

3.3. Strengths and Limitations
Across the included studies, hybrid phishing detection strategies generally demon-
strated strong predictive performance under their respective experimental settings (see
Table 4). Most models reported accuracies between approximately 95% and 99.9%, with
several achieving AUC values above 0.99 and false-positive rates below 3%. However, these
performance values should not be interpreted as directly comparable across studies because
the reviewed works used different datasets, class distributions, feature representations,
validation strategies, and baseline configurations. Therefore, the observed performance
gains are more appropriately interpreted as within-study improvements over the corre-
sponding baseline or competing models evaluated under the same experimental conditions.
For example, the hybrid CNN in [38] outperformed URL-only and HTML-only variants
within the same evaluation setting, SMOTE increased accuracy from 83% to 98% in [40],
and the ensembles in [41] and [47] outperformed their respective individual base classifiers.
However, the performance improvement reported in [40] should be interpreted cautiously
given the study’s low methodological quality score and limitations in evaluation reporting
and external validation. Apart from this, feature fusion, ensemble learning, and class bal-
ancing were recurrent strategies associated with improved performance within individual
studies. Models that combined semantic embeddings with sequential, statistical, visual, or
metadata features also demonstrated improved capability for handling homograph URLs,
shortened links, syntactic manipulation, and multimodal phishing content within their
evaluated datasets [39,43,52–55]. Large-scale evaluations involving hundreds of thousands
or millions of samples further provided evidence of scalability and performance stability
for the specific datasets and evaluation settings considered by those studies [39,43,47,55].
These findings indicate the potential benefits of hybridization, but they do not establish a
universal performance advantage across datasets or deployment environments. Despite
these strengths, several limitations were identified across the reviewed literature. Com-
putational complexity is a recurring concern, especially in deep ensembles, multimodal
pipelines, optimization-heavy frameworks, and systems that combine multiple explainabil-
ity components [43,44,46,48,49,51–55]. Higher training costs, memory requirements, and
inference latency may restrict real-time deployment, particularly in resource-constrained
environments. Many high-performing models also remain URL-centric, which may limit
their ability to detect content-rich, image-based, attachment-based, or voice-based phishing
attacks [43,45,47,50]. In addition, reliance on handcrafted features, static datasets, external
reputation services, or synchronized multimodal inputs may reduce robustness when
phishing tactics and data distributions evolve [44,47,50,52–55]. These limitations are consis-
tent with the quality appraisal in Section 3.1, which identified reproducibility reporting
and large-scale real-world validation as the most frequently under-reported aspects across
the included studies. So, the evidence suggests that hybridization can improve perfor-
mance within specific experimental settings, while questions concerning generalizability,
deployment efficiency, reproducibility, and robustness remain insufficiently addressed.
3.4. Challenges and Controversies
Despite the generally strong empirical results reported within individual studies,
several unresolved challenges remain in hybrid phishing detection. A central concern
is the trade-off between detection performance and computational efficiency. Complex
ensembles, multimodal pipelines, and adaptive frameworks may improve performance
over their respective baselines, but they also increase training costs, memory requirements,
inference latency, and deployment complexity, particularly in browser, mobile, and edge
environments [43–45,49,52–55]. Consequently, performance improvements reported under
https://doi.org/10.3390/computers15110746

## PAGE 15
Computers2026,15, 746 15 of 20
controlled experimental conditions do not necessarily translate into equivalent benefits in
resource-constrained or real-time deployment environments.
Generalization and dataset dependency also remain important challenges. Many stud-
ies rely on benchmark or curated datasets that may not fully represent evolving real-world
phishing patterns [38,39,47,48,52–55]. Data augmentation, synthetic sample generation,
and resampling may improve class balance and, in some cases, robustness, but they can also
introduce distributional artifacts or fail to represent genuinely unseen attacks [40,48,53,54].
In particular, the performance improvements reported by Alsubaei et al. [ 40] should be
interpreted cautiously because of limitations in methodological reporting, reproducibil-
ity, and broader validation. The use of separately collected multimodal datasets further
raises concerns regarding data alignment and evaluation realism [52,53]. Thus, reported
performance should be interpreted in relation to the dataset characteristics, modalities,
and evaluation designs of individual studies rather than as evidence of general superior-
ity across the literature. Temporal validation, cross-dataset evaluation, and adversarial
testing warrant greater attention when estimating practical performance under evolving
attack conditions.
Explainability represents another continuing challenge. Although several frameworks
incorporate SHAP , attention mechanisms, symbolic rules, Grad-CAM, or LLM-generated
explanations [48,49,52–55], many deep hybrid models still operate largely as black boxes.
Post-hoc explanations may improve transparency, but they do not necessarily guarantee
causal reliability or consistent interpretation by security analysts. Symbolic and rule-
based components may also become outdated as phishing tactics evolve [ 53,55]. These
observations indicate a need to evaluate not only whether explanations can be generated but
also whether they remain reliable, stable, and useful under changing attack distributions.
Other issues, including fairness, privacy, and continual adaptation, appear less ex-
tensively represented in the reviewed literature. Apart from a small number of lifecycle-
oriented or adaptive frameworks, limited attention was given to performance disparities
across user groups, privacy-preserving learning, and the risks associated with continual
model updates [49,53]. These topics should therefore be regarded as underexplored areas
within the reviewed evidence rather than as extensively established research gaps. More
broadly, the literature indicates a need to extend hybridization beyond model architec-
ture toward robust evaluation, drift monitoring, secure adaptation, and human-in-the-
loop validation. Such extensions may help bridge the gap between strong performance
under study-specific experimental conditions and sustainable deployment in dynamic
phishing environments.
To examine the influence of methodological quality on the review findings, a qual-
itative sensitivity analysis was undertaken by excluding Alsubaei et al. [ 40], the only
study classified as low quality (3.5/7), and reconsidering the evidence from the remaining
17 studies. The exclusion removed the study-specific performance improvements attributed
to SMOTE-based class balancing and hyperparameter optimization from the sensitivity
comparison. The principal challenges concerning computational efficiency, dataset depen-
dency, generalizability, explainability, and reproducibility remained represented across
other included studies [38,39,43–45,47–49,52–55]. Accordingly, the exclusion did not mate-
rially alter the principal qualitative themes of the review, although it reduced the evidence
supporting the specific performance gains reported by Alsubaei et al. [40].
3.5. Future Directions
The future research directions proposed in this study are informed by the limitations
and challenges identified in the reviewed literature, while also incorporating emerging
areas that were only lightly represented among the included studies. To address the
https://doi.org/10.3390/computers15110746

## PAGE 16
Computers2026,15, 746 16 of 20
accuracy-efficiency trade-off, future work should prioritize lightweight hybrid architec-
tures that reduce computational overhead while preserving the within-study performance
advantages of hybridization. Techniques such as model pruning, quantization, modular
late-fusion designs, and efficient ensemble construction may support real-time deployment
in resource-constrained environments. To address generalization and dataset dependency,
future studies should place greater emphasis on temporal validation, cross-dataset eval-
uation, and real-world testing. Continual learning and adaptive retraining mechanisms
may further improve robustness against evolving and previously unseen phishing at-
tacks. Replay-buffer-based online learning and controlled adaptation strategies could
also be investigated to support model updates while reducing the risk of catastrophic
forgetting or uncontrolled adaptation. These approaches should be evaluated using clearly
defined temporal and cross-domain settings rather than relying solely on performance
within a single benchmark dataset. The limitations of predominantly URL-centric detection
also motivate further investigation of multimodal hybrid frameworks that integrate URL,
HTML, visual, textual, and behavioral signals. Metadata, sender reputation, webpage
screenshots, attachment content, and missing-modality handling could be incorporated
to improve robustness in more realistic environments. However, multimodal integration
should be accompanied by explicit evaluation of data alignment, computational cost, and
the contribution of individual modalities to ensure that additional complexity provides
measurable practical benefits. To enhance explainability, future AI-based hybrid models
could incorporate interpretable AI components at both the feature and decision levels.
SHAP , Grad-CAM, attention mechanisms, symbolic reasoning, and analyst-oriented LLM-
generated explanations may provide complementary forms of interpretability [48,49,52–55].
Future evaluations should additionally examine explanation stability, fidelity, and useful-
ness to security analysts rather than treating the presence of an explanation mechanism
alone as evidence of trustworthy interpretation. Fairness-aware and privacy-preserving
hybrid detection also represent promising emerging research directions. Because these
topics received relatively limited attention among the included studies [ 49,53], future
research could investigate performance disparities across relevant user or data groups,
privacy-preserving learning mechanisms, and secure model adaptation. Such work would
extend the scope of hybrid phishing detection beyond predictive performance toward
responsible and trustworthy deployment. These directions are proposed as opportunities
for further investigation rather than as extensively established gaps directly demonstrated
by the current review. Finally, the increasing use of AI and large language models to
generate or facilitate phishing content creates an emerging security challenge. Although
this topic was only lightly represented in the reviewed literature, future studies could
investigate hybrid defenses capable of detecting semantically sophisticated, obfuscated,
and AI-generated phishing content. Diffusion-based adversarial augmentation, neuro-
symbolic reasoning, semantic analysis, and broader adversarial testing may provide useful
directions for evaluating robustness against such threats. Because the evidence base for
LLM-generated phishing within the included studies remains limited, these directions
should be considered emerging research opportunities rather than conclusions directly
established by the systematic review.
4. Conclusions
This systematic review examined 18 peer-reviewed studies on AI-based hybrid phish-
ing detection published between 2020 and June 2026 following the systematic literature
review guidance of Kitchenham and Charters [ 56]. The findings indicate that hybrid
approaches integrating multiple feature types, learning models, data modalities, and op-
erational components generally achieved strong detection performance and frequently
https://doi.org/10.3390/computers15110746

## PAGE 17
Computers2026,15, 746 17 of 20
outperformed the single-method baselines evaluated within individual studies. Recent
research also demonstrates a shift from URL-centered approaches toward multimodal,
explainable, adaptive, and deployment-oriented frameworks. Despite these advances,
several methodological and practical limitations remain. Many studies relied on bench-
mark or curated datasets, provided insufficient resources for reproducibility, and lacked
extensive cross-dataset or real-world validation. Computational complexity, inference
latency, and limited explainability further constrain practical deployment, particularly
in resource-limited environments. Consequently, high detection performance under con-
trolled experimental conditions should not be interpreted as evidence of equivalent effec-
tiveness against evolving real-world phishing threats. A qualitative sensitivity analysis
excluding Alsubaei et al. [40], the only low-quality study, indicated that the principal find-
ings remained supported by the remaining 17 studies. However, the exclusion reduced the
evidence supporting the specific performance improvements associated with SMOTE-based
resampling and hyperparameter optimization reported by that study. This finding high-
lights the importance of considering methodological quality when interpreting reported
detection performance. Future research should prioritize lightweight and scalable hybrid
architectures, broader multimodal integration, continual learning, and rigorous evaluation
through temporal testing, cross-dataset validation, adversarial assessment, and realistic
deployment scenarios. Greater attention is also needed to reproducibility, privacy, fairness,
robustness against AI-generated phishing, and interpretable decision-making. Addressing
these challenges will be essential for translating promising experimental performance into
reliable, adaptable, and sustainable phishing detection systems.
